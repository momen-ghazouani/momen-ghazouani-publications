Who Pays for Understanding?

Cheap output, costly verification, and the pollution problem of the AI era

Momen Ghazouani Founder CEO Of Setaleur Nov 24, 2026

In January 2026, Daniel Stenberg, the creator and lead maintainer of curl, shut down the project’s bug bounty program. Curl is a data transfer tool embedded in an enormous number of programs and devices, and its bounty, which had run since 2019, existed to pay researchers who found real security flaws. Stenberg closed it because the reports had become unmanageable. A growing share were generated with AI tools and described vulnerabilities that did not exist, written fluently enough that each one still had to be read, tested and refuted by a very small team. In a keynote at FOSDEM a few weeks later, he estimated that in the past roughly one report in six had turned out to be a genuine security flaw, while now it was closer to one in twenty or thirty.

Two details of this story matter more than the headline. The first is that Stenberg is not an opponent of AI. He has said that skilled people using AI tools have found real bugs in curl, and that the problem is less the technology than the incentives of the people using it carelessly. The second is the shape of the cost. Writing a plausible but false vulnerability report now takes almost no effort. Establishing that it is false takes a trained engineer an hour or more. The saving goes to one person and the bill goes to another.

This essay argues that this small episode is a preview of a general problem. When the cost of producing outputs collapses while the cost of verifying and understanding them does not, the difference does not vanish. It accumulates somewhere, usually in the shared systems that many people depend on and no one owns: codebases, classrooms, courts, scientific literature, the data from which future models learn. I will call what accumulates pollution, and I will use the word in its technical sense, the way economists do. It is a cost imposed on third parties that the producer never pays.

The framing matters, so let me be precise about what I am not claiming. I am not claiming that every technological advance carries an equal and opposite harm, as if the world kept a ledger that must balance. That is a slogan borrowed from physics, and it implies a fatalism the evidence does not support. Pollution is not the opposite of progress. It is a side effect of production whose size depends on whether institutions exist to measure it and assign its cost. Industrial societies produced enormous quantities of pollution, and they reduced it not by hoping but by building things: instruments to measure it, standards to define it, regulators to enforce those standards, and legal principles that made the polluter responsible. The same path is open here, and the second half of this essay is about what it would look like.

1. A Short Account of the Absorption Problem

Any output of an AI system, whether a block of code, a legal brief, a lesson plan, a chemical compound or a mathematical proof, has no value until human institutions can do four things with it. They must verify it, understand it well enough to trust it, integrate it into existing practice, and take responsibility for what follows. I call the capacity to do these four things the absorption layer, and the claim I want to defend is that this layer, and not the production of outputs, is the real constraint on how a technology changes the world.

The examples are everywhere once you look. A candidate drug is not a treatment until it has passed trials, regulators, manufacturing, clinical guidelines and liability law. A proposed design is not a bridge until standards bodies, inspectors, contractors and insurers have all had their say. A proposed mathematical result is not knowledge until referees and the research community have worked through it. Each of those is a system, built over decades, made of people, rules and incentives.

History suggests that this layer, and not the invention itself, sets the pace. When electric motors first reached factories, productivity did not leap. The economic historian Paul David showed that the gains took a generation to arrive, because factories had to be redesigned around the new technology rather than simply fitted with it. In 1987 the economist Robert Solow observed that computers were visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics, and again the explanation was largely organizational: work had to be reorganized before the machines could pay off. In both cases the technology was ready. The absorbing systems were the constraint.

This idea is usually presented as a brake. Absorption is slow, so transformation is slow. The argument of this essay concerns the other side of it. What happens when output arrives faster than the layer can take it in? An absorption layer does not merely slow things down when it is overloaded. It degrades, and it degrades quietly. Reviewers begin to skim. Institutions begin to approve what they cannot check. People begin to trust what they have no means of examining. Nothing announces this change, because from the outside everything still appears to function.

2. The Asymmetry

The core of the problem is an asymmetry between two costs. The cost of producing a plausible output, a paragraph, a patch, a citation, a report, has fallen toward zero and keeps falling. The cost of verifying that output has barely moved, because verification is bound by things that do not scale: human attention, human expertise, and the slow business of actually understanding what is in front of you.

The software engineer Alberto Brandolini put a version of this into a well-known saying in 2013: the effort needed to refute nonsense is an order of magnitude larger than the effort needed to produce it. It was a remark about misinformation, but it describes the economics of any domain where fluent output is cheap and checking is not. Generative systems did not invent this asymmetry. They widened it dramatically, and they widened it in domains that used to be protected by the simple fact that producing anything convincing required real effort.

Why does verification not get cheap along with production? Because to verify something you must understand it, at least well enough to say where it could be wrong, and understanding is not a mechanical step that can be sped up arbitrarily. A reviewer approving a change to a codebase must hold in mind what the code is supposed to do, what it actually does, and what it might break elsewhere. A judge reading a brief must know the law well enough to notice that a cited case does not say what it is claimed to say. A teacher reading an essay must know the subject well enough to tell insight from fluent filler. These are acts of comprehension, and comprehension is slow.

There is also a subtler difficulty: people underestimate this cost. In 2025 the research group METR ran a randomized trial in which sixteen experienced open-source developers completed 246 real tasks in repositories they knew well, with AI tools allowed on some tasks and forbidden on others. Before starting, the developers predicted that the tools would make them about 24 percent faster. In fact they took about 19 percent longer with the tools, and even afterward they believed they had been about 20 percent faster. The authors were careful to say that this does not show AI slows down developers in general: the sample was small, the setting was specific, and the tools were those of early 2025, which have since changed. But the study illustrates something that does generalize. Time spent reading, checking and correcting machine output is easy to overlook, because it feels like part of the work. The cost is real and it is hidden, and costs that are hidden are exactly the ones that end up unpaid.

This is where the economic idea of an externality becomes useful. An externality is a cost of an activity that falls on people who did not choose it and are not compensated for it. A factory that discharges waste into a river saves money on disposal and passes the cost to everyone downstream. When someone submits an unverified AI-generated report to a maintainer, a brief to a court, or a piece of work to a teacher, the same structure appears. The submitter captures the benefit of cheap production, and the reviewer pays for the verification. The commons being polluted is not water or air but attention and trust.

Commons problems have a well-understood dynamic. Each individual producer has a small incentive to cut a corner, since the damage is spread across everyone. In aggregate, the corners add up to a degraded shared resource, and by the time the degradation is visible it is expensive to reverse. This is why the problem will not solve itself through individual good behavior, even though individual good behavior matters.

3. What Spreads Without Being Understood

There is an intuitive version of this argument that I want to reject, because it is wrong and easy to refute. It says: if the community cannot understand something, it cannot spread. Code that no one can read will not be adopted. A text that no teacher can evaluate will not be taught. The system will protect itself.

It will not, because things spread without being understood all the time. Modern software is built on layers of components that most developers never read. The attack on the xz compression library, discovered in March 2024, is a vivid case. A backdoor was inserted into a widely used piece of infrastructure by someone who had spent years building trust with a lone, overworked maintainer. It was found largely by accident, when an engineer noticed that remote logins had become slightly slower than they should be. Nothing about this episode involved AI. It shows what thin human attention costs in a system that everyone relies on, and it shows that the protective mechanism of “someone must have looked at this” is far weaker than we assume.

So the accurate version of the claim is different. Unintelligible things do spread. They spread without being understood, and the understanding debt accumulates silently until something forces a reckoning. I use the phrase understanding debt by analogy with technical debt in software. It is the gap between what a system contains and what the people responsible for it can explain. Like financial debt, it is not inherently bad. Prudent borrowing is how most progress happens, and no one understands every layer of the tools they use. The danger lies in debt that is invisible, unmeasured and compounding. When code written by a machine is built upon by more code written by a machine, and no human ever read the foundation, the interest is paid later, in incidents that no one can diagnose and systems that no one can safely change.

A fair objection is that we all depend on things we do not verify, and that this is perfectly rational. The philosopher John Hardwig made the point forcefully in 1985 in a paper on epistemic dependence: in a world of specialized knowledge, almost everything any of us knows rests on trusting others. I trust the encyclopedia, the calculator and the pharmacist without checking their work. This is not a flaw in human reasoning. It is how knowledge scales.

But look at why that trust is justified. It rests on infrastructure: editors, peer review, professional licensing, reputations that can be damaged, legal liability, and, crucially, amortization. An encyclopedia entry is checked once by a few people and then read by millions. A compiler is verified through long use by a vast community, and the same program behaves the same way every time. The cost of verification is spread across all users, which is what makes it affordable.

The output of a generative model usually lacks this property. It is produced fresh for a particular occasion. There is no edition that a million people have read and corrected. Its verification cannot be shared, so it must be paid again each time by whoever relies on it, or else not paid at all. A track record for the model as a whole is useful, because it gives a base rate of error, but a base rate cannot tell you which particular output is the wrong one. This is the real difference between trusting a calculator and trusting a model, and it is why the “you also trust the encyclopedia” reply, while correct as far as it goes, does not settle the matter. The question is not whether we may trust what we cannot check. The question is whether the infrastructure that makes such trust rational exists for this new kind of output, and for the most part it does not yet.

4. The Chain of Understanding

Seen this way, the examples that look unrelated turn out to share a structure. In each, a body of shared knowledge is maintained by a chain of people, and each link in the chain works only because the previous link was understood by someone.

Consider open-source software, which is the clearest case. The model works because of layered checks: an author writes a change, a reviewer examines it, a maintainer accepts it, and users rely on the result, with each stage catching some of what the previous one missed. If the first link becomes nearly free while the second remains expensive, the chain can fail in two ways. It can jam, as at curl, where the volume of low-quality input consumes the maintainers’ limited time and good reports get buried with the bad. Or it can thin, as at xz, where reviewers approve what they cannot properly examine. Both failures are consequences of the same imbalance, and both are worse for projects that are small, underfunded and heavily relied upon, which describes a remarkable share of the software that modern life depends on.

Consider next the classroom. Education is a chain in which each link must be understood by the next: a researcher produces knowledge, an author records it, a teacher understands it, and a student learns it. A teacher cannot teach what the teacher cannot evaluate. If an AI system produces an explanation, a lesson plan or a worked solution, it has instructional value only to the extent that a competent person can check it, and a confident but subtly wrong explanation passed to thirty students is the kind of error that propagates before anyone notices.

There is a second problem in education that is just as serious. For generations the student essay has served as a proxy: a piece of text that gave evidence that the student had understood something. That proxy has been broken. In controlled studies, teachers have been unable to reliably tell machine-written essays from student work. One study of high school teachers found that they identified the machine-written essay in a pair about 70 percent of the time, and that their confidence did not predict their accuracy. A German study found that novice and experienced teachers alike could not identify AI-generated texts among student essays. The point is not that teachers should become detectives. The point is that when fluent text no longer implies understanding, the signal that the whole system used to certify learning has lost its meaning, and everything downstream of the certificate, such as the hiring manager, the licensing board and the patient, inherits the uncertainty. Students who use AI to explain things to themselves are doing something valuable. The failure occurs when fluency is mistaken for comprehension, by the student, the teacher or the institution.

Consider the courtroom. In 2023, in the case Mata v. Avianca, lawyers submitted a brief citing cases that did not exist, generated by a chatbot, and a federal judge sanctioned them. That was treated at the time as a cautionary anecdote. It is now a genre. A database kept by the researcher Damien Charlotin, as summarized by a University of North Carolina law library in June 2026, recorded more than a thousand cases in United States courts alone involving improper use of generative AI in filings. Even specialized legal research tools, which are designed to reduce the problem, were found in a Stanford study to produce incorrect or misleading answers in a substantial minority of queries, roughly one in six to one in three depending on the tool. And the verification burden has fallen where the theory predicts: on opposing counsel, on overworked clerks and judges, and even on the researcher who maintains the database, who has said that keeping up with the flow of new cases is becoming too much. The courts, notably, have started to respond in exactly the way pollution policy would suggest. In late May 2026 the Florida Supreme Court amended its rules to require that filings verify that the cited authorities exist and are accurately cited, and some courts have begun ordering the offending firms to pay the opposing side’s costs. In one Illinois case a firm and a partner were ordered to pay a combined 59,500 dollars to the firm that discovered the fake citations. That is the polluter-pays principle, rediscovered by judges working with the tools they have.

Finally, consider data itself. Future models learn from text, code and images, and a growing share of that material is now produced by earlier models. Researchers reported in Nature in 2024 that training models indiscriminately on the output of other models can cause progressive degradation, with rare cases and unusual features dropping out of the distribution first. This finding needs a caution: later work has shown that the effect can be avoided when synthetic data is added alongside real data rather than replacing it. But the underlying point holds. The pool of human-generated, verified material is a commons, and an expensive one, and its protection will have to be deliberate.

None of these four cases is identical to the others, but the pattern is the same. Production became cheap, verification stayed expensive, and the cost landed on a shared resource maintained by a chain of people who were not asked.

5. Objections

An essay that cannot be wrong is not worth much, so let me take the strongest objections seriously.

The first is that AI will solve the problem it creates, by verifying its own outputs. There is real truth in this. In domains with a reliable checker, such as formal proofs and software with strong test suites, machine verification is powerful and improving, and the stories above include cases where AI-assisted work by skilled people found genuine flaws. I expect AI to become an important part of the verification layer. But the response has limits. Where no formal checker exists, a model verifying a model is still a source of judgment that someone must trust, and models trained in similar ways may share blind spots, so that their errors correlate and agreement between them proves less than it seems. And there is an old regress at the center of it. In his 1984 Turing Award lecture, Ken Thompson showed that a compiler could be made to insert a backdoor that no inspection of the source code would reveal, and drew the lesson that you cannot trust code you did not create yourself, and by extension, the tools that create it. Someone must stand at the end of every chain of verification and be accountable, and that someone has to be able to understand what they are vouching for. Automating the checking is useful. It does not remove the need for the people at the end.

The second objection is that markets will correct the problem, because unreliable outputs will lose value and users will learn to discount them. Sometimes this happens. But externalities are, by definition, costs that the price system does not capture, which is why they persist until something outside the market intervenes. The curl bounty did not end because the market found an equilibrium. It ended because a maintainer decided he could not afford it. Courts did not wait for a market solution either. They changed the rules.

The third objection is that humans have always relied on things they did not understand, and civilization survived. This is true, and I have said so. The difference lies in the rate and in the infrastructure of trust. Past technologies that were used without being fully understood generally arrived with slower adoption, with institutions that had time to develop, and with outputs that were stable enough to be tested once and relied on repeatedly. The new situation combines unprecedented speed, outputs that are novel each time, and a verification system built for a world in which producing a convincing artifact was itself a costly signal of effort and competence. Those assumptions no longer hold, and the institutions that depended on them need to be rebuilt, not merely defended.

A fourth point, less an objection than a caution: some of the friction that would reduce pollution would also reduce real benefits. The same tools that flood courts with bad filings also help people who cannot afford a lawyer to navigate the legal system, and the same tools that overwhelm maintainers also help newcomers make their first contribution to a project. A policy that treats all machine-assisted work as suspect would waste something valuable. The aim is not to slow production down. It is to make the costs of production visible and to place them on those who benefit.

6. What the History of Pollution Suggests

Industrial pollution was not solved by a single measure. It was addressed by a cluster of institutional inventions, each of which has a counterpart in the situation described here.

First, measurement. You cannot manage what you cannot see. Societies began to control pollution when they learned to measure it, with monitoring stations, standardized tests and public reporting. The equivalent here is to measure understanding debt and verification load: how much code in a critical project was written without human review, what share of submissions to a maintainer or a court are unverified, how much reviewer time they consume. Right now these costs are anecdotes. They need to become statistics.

Second, the polluter pays. The principle that those who create a cost should bear it is the most powerful tool in environmental policy, and early signs of it already exist in the cost-shifting orders that some courts have issued. Platforms and institutions can adopt analogues: requiring submitters to attest that they have verified their work and to reproduce claims before filing them, attaching consequences to repeated unverified submissions, and making the person who benefits from cheap production responsible for a share of the cost of checking it.

Third, disclosure and provenance. Pollution regulation depends on knowing where waste comes from. In the same way, institutions should be able to tell what was machine-generated, what was reviewed and by whom. Curl’s own policy of requiring disclosure of AI use in reports was an early example. Provenance does not resolve quality, since AI-assisted work can be excellent and unassisted work can be poor, but it lets reviewers allocate their scarce attention rationally.

Fourth, investment in the commons. Clean water is a public good that someone must pay to maintain, and so is the verified understanding that open-source software, scientific literature and legal databases rest on. If the world is going to rely on a small number of volunteer maintainers and reviewers, it should not be surprising when they are overwhelmed. Funding the review layer, in the same way that we fund water treatment, is not a subsidy to the unproductive. It is maintenance of the infrastructure on which production rests.

Fifth, design for checkability. The best response to expensive verification is output that is cheap to verify. A formal proof can be checked by a machine. A code change accompanied by tests, a clear description and a minimal footprint can be reviewed in minutes instead of hours. A claim accompanied by its reasoning and its sources can be evaluated rather than merely believed. Systems should be built and rewarded for producing artifacts of this kind, so that the cost of verification is lowered at the source, rather than imposed on the reader.

Sixth, human capacity. The reviewers, editors, teachers, auditors and judges who sit between machine output and its consequences need training suited to the new situation, including how to examine output efficiently, how to recognize the characteristic failures of generative systems, and how to decide what deserves deep scrutiny. This is a skill, and it will become one of the most valuable skills there is.

Seventh, and last, assessment that measures understanding rather than output. In education and in professional certification, we should shift toward forms of assessment in which understanding has to be demonstrated in ways that fluent text cannot fake: oral examination, work done in view of the examiner, tasks that require explaining and defending choices. This is not a retreat from technology. It is a recognition that the old proxy has stopped working and that a new one must be built.

7. Conclusion

The conventional story of technological progress asks how much more we can produce. The harder and more important question is how much more we can understand, verify and take responsibility for. Production has become cheap. Understanding has not, and it is what turns production into something we can safely depend on.

That asymmetry is not a reason for pessimism, and it is not a law of nature. It is a design problem, of a kind that societies have solved before. Rivers became cleaner because people decided that clean rivers were worth the cost, and then built the instruments, rules and institutions to make it so. The same decision is available now, in a different domain. A maintainer, a teacher, a judge, a reviewer and an editor are not obstacles to the technology. They are the places where the technology becomes safe to use, and they are the scarce resource.

The question that gives this essay its title is therefore a practical one. Someone will pay for the understanding that makes machine output trustworthy. The only open question is whether the cost will be paid deliberately, by those who benefit and through institutions built for the purpose, or whether it will be left to accumulate on the shoulders of overworked individuals until something breaks. We can still choose, and the sooner we begin building, the cheaper the choice will be.
